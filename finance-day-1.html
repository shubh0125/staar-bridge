Finance · Day 1

Banking Basics

“If someone handed you $500, where would you put it to keep it safe and have complete access?". Most people would say “under their pillow” or “in their phone case”; however, oftentimes the safest place is not with an individual but rather a third party. A bank, a financial institution licensed to accept deposits, provide loans, and manage everyday transactions, would be the best place for one to hold their money.

This brings us to one of the most fundamental topics: how to manage and work a bank account.

What is a bank account actually?

A bank account is a financial arrangement where a bank or credit union holds, tracks, and manages your money. In simple words, a bank is a safety deposit box that also happens to pay your bills for you. You put money in, the bank keeps it secure, and you can access it anytime through a card, an app, or a check. Accounts are federally insured up to $250,000, meaning it's genuinely safer than cash in your pocket.

Two of the most foundational accounts that most adults should know about are a checking account and a savings account.

A crucial idea that is a part of almost every account opened within a bank is interest. Banks don't just hold your money in a vault; instead, they lend a portion of it out to other people for things such as a car or mortgage. Once the borrower repays the initial amount borrowed, they also pay a certain interest on that. A portion of that interest will go right into your pocket. Let's break this down into simpler terms.

  1. You deposit $500 into a savings account
  2. Bank pools it together with thousands of other deposits
  3. Bank lends it to a homebuyer as a mortgage
  4. Homebuyer repays monthly principal + interest, over 15 years
  5. You get a small cut as the interest on your savings

As the depositor (you in the current situation): The moment you deposit the money, the bank is legally allowed to lend most of it back out. That's how banks afford to pay you interest. Banks charge the borrower a much higher interest rate than they pay you. If a mortgage rate is 7%, your savings account might pay 2-4%. That gap, also known as the spread, is the bank's profit. You’re taking on essentially zero risk, since your deposit is federally insured, in exchange for a small, safe return, while the bank takes on the risks.

As the person buying the house: The mortgage you get isn't coming from some money the bank keeps sitting in a vault. It's coming from other people's deposits, pooled together. That's actually why your credit score, income, and down payment matter so much to the lender: they're not handing you their own money, but rather a slice of thousands of other depositors' money, and they need 100% confidence that you'll pay it back before they take that risk. Every payment you make each month is split: part goes to the bank as its profit and interest to depositors (interest), and part goes back into that same pool that depositors as you drew from.

Opening your first account, Step by Step

  1. Pick a bank or credit Union: Credit Unions are something worth comparing, since they often carry lower fees.
  2. Bring 3 things: a government ID, your Social Security card or number, and proof of address (a piece of mail works)
  3. Decide checking, savings, or both: Most people open both on day one
  4. Make an opening deposit: Often as little as $25, sometimes $0 depending on the institution
  5. Set up your debit card PIN and mobile banking app right there in the branch

Bank or Credit Union? First Step

A bank is a business. Its customers are you, but its owners are shareholders, and the bank's core job is to make those shareholders money. Every fee, every interest rate spread, every monthly maintenance charge is partly there to generate profit for people who have nothing to do with your local branch.

A credit union is a nonprofit, and its members are its owners. Open an account, and you're not just a customer. Because there's no separate group of shareholders demanding a profit, credit unions can pass the savings straight back to members as lower fees and better interest rates. That's the actual reason credit unions tend to win on cost.

A few things worth checking before deciding:

  • See if you're eligible for a credit union before assuming you're not. Most credit unions today have wide requirements such as living, working, worshipping, or going to school in a certain area.
  • Compare the fee schedule. Monthly maintenance fees, minimum balance requirements, overdraft fees, and out-of-network ATM fees are all public information on any bank or credit union's website and are a must-read.
  • Weigh how often you'll need a physical branch. Big banks generally win here with more branches, more ATMs, more travel-friendly apps. Credit unions often don’t.

Our recommendation for a first account is if a credit union you're eligible for has lower fees and decent app reviews, it's usually the better starting point. Banks make more sense once someone needs heavy branch access, frequent cash deposits, extensive travel, or a specific product a local credit union doesn't offer.

How a Debit Card Actually Works

A debit card pulls money directly from your checking account the moment you use it. Unlike a credit card, you can't spend money you don't have.

A few things worth knowing cold before your first swipe:

  • Never share your card number or PIN with anyone, including "bank employees" who call or text asking for it. A real bank would never ask for your PIN
  • Check your account app regularly; catching a mistake or fraud charge early is far easier to fix than catching it late

Overdraft fees: #1 fee most people miss

Some banks still let a purchase go through even without enough money in the account, and then they end up charging a fee for the overcharge. A $60 purchase you didn't know you couldn't afford can quietly turn into $95: $45 balance minus a $60 charge leaves you at –$15, and then a typical $35 overdraft fee turns $15 in debt into a $50 debt.

The fix is simple: turn on low-balance alerts in your banking app (most will text you when you're close to $0), and consider opting out of "overdraft coverage" entirely. Without it, the purchase just gets declined instead of costing you a fee.

Why We're Starting Here

Banking is the first lesson in our series for a reason: budgeting, credit, avoiding predatory loans, all depend on someone actually having a safe place to put their money first. Get that foundation right, and everything after it gets a little easier to build.
