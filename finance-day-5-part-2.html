Taxes, Part 2: Everything Else Worth Knowing

Here's a number that surprises almost everyone the first time they hear it: for the 2025 tax year, a single filer doesn't owe federal income tax on the first $15,000 they earn. That's the standard deduction, applied automatically, no paperwork required to claim it. If you're working part-time or entry-level hours, there's a real chance most or all of what you earned this year falls under that line entirely.

The Math That Actually Determines What You Owe

That example isn't a best-case scenario; it's realistic for someone working part-time hours near minimum wage. The core mechanic: your employer withholds an estimated amount from every paycheck all year, based on a rough guess of what you'll owe. For most entry-level earners, that guess overshoots, because the standard deduction wipes out most of the tax bill. The difference between what got withheld and what you actually owed comes back to you as a refund, but only if you file. Skip filing, and that money just stays with the IRS permanently.

W-2 vs. 1099: Why It Changes What You Owe

  • W-2 means you're a regular employee; taxes are already being withheld from each paycheck automatically, which is why the refund scenario above is so common.
  • 1099 means you're an independent contractor (gig work, freelance); nothing gets withheld automatically, so you're responsible for setting money aside yourself, and you may owe quarterly estimated taxes rather than getting a refund. If you do any 1099 work, a rough rule of thumb is to set aside 20–25% of what you earn for taxes as you go, so filing season isn't a shock.

The Credit Almost Nobody Tells Former Foster Youth About

This is worth its own section because it's genuinely underused: the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) normally requires childless filers to be at least 19 years old. But under federal law, qualified former foster youth and qualified homeless youth can claim it starting at age 18, no student status required, no complicated extra paperwork beyond checking a box on your return. "Qualified former foster youth" means you were in foster care at any point after turning 14 and consented to your status being shared for this purpose.

The IRS itself has noted that roughly 20% of people eligible for the EITC nationally don't claim it, usually because they don't know they qualify. Given that this exact exception exists specifically for people in the population this program serves, it's worth checking every single year. The exact credit amount changes annually based on income, but it can add several hundred dollars to a refund for someone who'd otherwise get little or nothing extra. Check the IRS's EITC Assistant tool, or ask directly at a VITA site; they're trained to catch this exact exception.

Why "Free File" Actually Means Free

  • IRS Free File (irs.gov/freefile): anyone who earned less than $89,000 in 2025 can use guided, name-brand tax software at no cost, directly through the IRS. This isn't a stripped-down version; it's the real product, free because of an agreement between the IRS and tax software companies.
  • VITA (Volunteer Income Tax Assistance) offers free, in-person help from IRS-certified volunteers.
  • IRS Direct File — a newer option letting some filers submit directly to the IRS for free, without third-party software at all.

None of these require handing your information to a paid preparer who takes a cut of your refund, which matters, because paid preparers targeting refund-eligible filers are a real, well-documented pattern worth avoiding entirely when free, equally legitimate options exist.

Don't Forget Ohio

Federal taxes get most of the attention, but Ohio also requires a separate state income tax return. The good news: most Free File providers let you file both your federal and Ohio returns together in the same session, so this usually isn't extra work; just make sure to complete both before considering yourself done.
