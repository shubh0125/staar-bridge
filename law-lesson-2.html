Your First Paycheck: Know What You're Owed

You get your first paycheck. The number's smaller than you expected. Is that normal, or did something actually go wrong?

Most of the time it's normal. But not always. Wage theft — getting paid less than you're legally owed — is way more common than people think. It could be a missed overtime calculation, a manager who "forgets" to pay for a shift, or a pay stub that doesn't add up. Here's what Ohio and federal law actually guarantee you, and how to tell the difference between a normal paycheck and a problem.

1. Your pay stub has to show real information now

Here's something brand new: starting in 2025, Ohio's Pay Stub Protection Act requires every employer to give you a written or electronic pay statement every payday. Before this law, Ohio was one of the only states where your employer could legally hand you a paycheck with zero explanation of how they got that number.

Now, every pay stub has to include:

  • Your name and address, and your employer's name.
  • Your total gross pay (before anything is taken out) and net pay (what you actually take home).
  • An itemized list of everything added or deducted — taxes, insurance, anything else — with what each one is for.
  • The pay date and the period it covers.
  • If you're paid hourly: total hours worked, your hourly rate, and how many of those hours were overtime.

If you're not getting this, you have a specific right to act: send your employer a written request for it. They have 10 business days to comply. If they don't, you can report it to the state (details below).

2. Minimum wage: know your actual number

As of January 1, 2026, Ohio's minimum wage is:

  • $11.00/hour for most workers.
  • $5.50/hour for tipped workers (though tips are supposed to bring you up to at least $11/hour total — if they don't, your employer has to make up the difference).

There's an exception worth knowing: if you work for a small business — one that grosses $405,000 or less a year — or you're 14 or 15 years old, the rate that applies to you is the federal minimum wage instead, which is $7.25/hour. Ohio's minimum wage also goes up almost every January to keep up with inflation, so the number you're owed isn't static — always worth a quick check at the start of a new year.

3. Overtime: the 40-hour rule

If you work more than 40 hours in a single work week, you're generally owed time-and-a-half — 1.5 times your regular hourly rate — for every hour past 40. This is federal law (the Fair Labor Standards Act), and Ohio follows the same rule for most employers.

A couple of things people get wrong:

  • It's based on hours in a week, not a pay period. Working 60 hours one week and 20 the next doesn't average out — the 60-hour week still owes you 20 hours of overtime.
  • Being paid "salary" doesn't automatically mean no overtime. Plenty of salaried jobs still legally qualify for overtime — it depends on your specific job duties and pay level, not just the word "salary" on your offer letter.

4. "At-will employment" — what it actually means

Ohio is an at-will employment state. In plain terms: your employer can generally let you go at any time, for almost any reason, without warning — and you can also quit at any time, for any reason. Neither side owes the other advance notice unless a contract says otherwise.

But "at-will" has real limits people often miss:

  • Your employer can't fire you for an illegal reason — discrimination based on things like race, sex, religion, or disability, for example.
  • Your employer can't fire you in retaliation for reporting wage theft, filing a safety complaint, or exercising a legal right. That protection exists specifically so people aren't afraid to report the exact problems this article covers.

5. Wage theft red flags

Wage theft isn't always obvious. Watch for:

  • Off-the-clock work — being asked to clock out but keep working, or arrive early/stay late unpaid.
  • Missing overtime — working over 40 hours and getting straight-time pay instead of time-and-a-half.
  • Illegal deductions — money taken out for things like broken equipment or register shortages, in ways that push you below minimum wage.
  • Tip theft — a manager or owner taking a cut of tips that are legally yours.
  • Being misclassified — called an "independent contractor" when you're actually being treated like a regular employee (set schedule, told exactly how to do the job, no real independence). This matters because contractors don't get minimum wage or overtime protections that employees do.
  • A final paycheck that never comes, or comes short, after you quit or get let go.

The best defense against all of these: keep your own log of hours worked, every shift, in your phone or a notebook. If your pay stub and your own record ever disagree, you have something concrete to point to.

6. What to actually do if something looks wrong

  1. Check your own hours log against your pay stub first. Sometimes it's a simple payroll error that gets fixed with one conversation.
  2. Raise it with your employer or HR, in writing if possible. Many issues get resolved at this stage.
  3. If it's a missing or incomplete pay stub, send a written request — remember, they have 10 business days to respond.
  4. If that doesn't fix it, file a complaint. You have two real options, and you can use either:
    • Ohio Bureau of Wage and Hour: com.ohio.gov/WageAndHourBureau — handles Ohio minimum wage and pay stub complaints.
    • U.S. Department of Labor, Wage and Hour Division: dol.gov/agencies/whd — handles federal overtime and minimum wage complaints, and covers a lot of employers Ohio's state law doesn't reach.

Don't wait too long to act — wage claims have time limits, so the sooner you raise a real problem, the stronger your case.

Quick path: compare your hours log to your pay stub → raise it with your employer, in writing if you can → if it's resolved, you're done — if not (or you get no response within 10 business days), file a complaint with the Ohio Bureau of Wage and Hour or the U.S. DOL.

Before your next paycheck, do these two things

  1. Start a simple hours log today — date, time in, time out, every shift, starting from your very first day at any job.
  2. Actually read your pay stub once you get one — check the hours, the rate, and the math, instead of just glancing at the total.

Where to get real help

  • Ohio Bureau of Wage and Hour — com.ohio.gov/WageAndHourBureau — Ohio wage and pay stub complaints, or call (614) 644-2239.
  • U.S. Dept. of Labor Wage and Hour Division — dol.gov/agencies/whd — federal minimum wage and overtime complaints.

A paycheck that doesn't add up isn't something to just shrug off. You're not being difficult by checking the math — you're doing exactly what the law says you're allowed to do.

This article is for general educational purposes and isn't a substitute for legal advice. Wage and hour rates and rules can change — always verify current numbers with the Ohio Department of Commerce or the U.S. Department of Labor before relying on them.
