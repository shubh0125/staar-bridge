The Scams Targeting People Your Age

Here's something that surprises most people: it's not seniors who report losing money to scams the most — it's people in their 20s. Federal data consistently shows young adults report fraud more often than any other age group, and it almost always starts in the same place: social media, a text message, or an online listing.

The good news is that most scams follow a small number of repeatable patterns. Once you can recognize the pattern, you can spot the scam before it costs you anything.

1. The pattern almost every scam shares

Nearly every scam — no matter what it's disguised as — relies on some combination of these:

  • Urgency. "Act now or you'll lose this opportunity/get evicted/be arrested." Real institutions rarely demand an instant decision.
  • An unusual payment method. Gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or payment apps to a stranger. No legitimate company, landlord, or government agency ever asks to be paid in gift cards. That single fact stops a huge share of scams cold.
  • Contact you didn't initiate. A message, call, or "job offer" that came to you out of nowhere, especially through social media or text.
  • A request for information a legitimate business already has — your Social Security number, bank login, or a one-time verification code.

2. The scams most likely to actually hit you

Job scams. You get a message — often through social media or even a school email — offering a job that pays well for little work, sometimes before you've even applied anywhere. They "hire" you fast, then ask you to buy equipment, cash a check and wire back part of it, or pay an "onboarding fee." A real employer never asks you to pay them to get hired, and never asks you to deposit a check and send part of the money back.

Online shopping scams. An ad on social media for a great deal, often on a site you've never heard of. You pay, and the item either never arrives or isn't what was advertised. Before buying from an unfamiliar site, a quick search for "[site name] scam reviews" takes seconds and can save you the money.

Apartment/rental listing scams. Since you now know what a real lease looks like, watch for the opposite: a "landlord" who's conveniently out of town, asks for a deposit or first month's rent by wire transfer or gift card before you've even seen the unit in person, and won't do a video call or in-person tour. If you can't verify the person is the real property owner or manager, don't send money.

Imposter scams. Someone contacts you claiming to be your bank, a government agency, a delivery company, or even a friend, asking you to "verify" information or click a link. Real organizations don't ask for your password or a one-time code over text or phone — if you're unsure, hang up and call the organization back using the number on their official website, not a number they gave you.

Prize and sweepstakes scams. You're told you won something you never entered — but you have to pay a "fee" or "taxes" first to claim it. You can't legally be required to pay to receive a real prize.

3. What to actually do if you think you're being scammed

  1. Stop responding immediately. Don't engage further, even to argue or ask questions.
  2. Don't send money or information, even if they threaten consequences for not complying.
  3. Verify independently. If someone claims to be your bank, landlord, or employer, contact them directly using a number or website you already know is real — not one the message gave you.
  4. Report it, even if you didn't lose money — reports help law enforcement spot patterns and shut scams down faster.

Where to actually report a scam

  • reportfraud.ftc.gov — the federal government's official scam reporting site. Takes a few minutes, and you'll get tailored next steps based on what happened.
  • Ohio Attorney General's Consumer Protection Section — ohioattorneygeneral.gov/consumer, or call (800) 282-0515.

Before you're ever targeted, remember this

The one rule that covers almost every scam on this list: no legitimate company, landlord, or government agency will ever ask you to pay them in gift cards. If you hear that request, the conversation is over — whoever you're talking to isn't who they say they are.

This article is for general educational purposes and isn't a substitute for legal advice. Scam tactics evolve constantly — always verify current warnings with the FTC or the Ohio Attorney General's office.
