Credit and Credit Scores

A landlord runs your credit before deciding whether to rent to you. A cell phone carrier runs it before giving you a phone plan without a deposit. A car dealership runs it before deciding your interest rate. All of that happens before you've ever taken out a loan on purpose, and most people don't find out how the system works until one of those moments has already gone badly.

A credit score is really just a number, between 300 and 850, that tries to predict one thing: how likely you are to pay back money you borrow.

What Actually Goes Into the Number

A FICO score, the most commonly used scoring model, is built from five factors, and they are not weighted equally:

  • Payment history (35%): do you pay on time, every time? This is the single biggest factor by far.
  • Amounts owed (30%): how much of your available credit you're actually using. Maxing out a card hurts you even if you pay it off eventually.
  • Length of credit history (15%): how long your accounts have existed. This is the one factor that only improves with time, which is exactly why starting early (even small) matters.
  • New credit (10%): opening several new accounts in a short window looks risky to lenders.
  • Credit mix (10%): having a mix of account types (a credit card and a loan, for example) helps slightly, but it's the smallest factor and never worth forcing.

The one sentence worth remembering: on-time payments and low balances account for 65% of your entire score. Everything else is a rounding error by comparison.

Why the Number Actually Follows You Around

A 650 credit score versus a 750 one isn't just about getting approved or denied — it changes the price of everything you borrow. On a $20,000 car loan, someone with a strong score might get an interest rate several points lower than someone with a weak one, which can mean thousands of dollars difference over the life of the loan for borrowing the same amount of money. Some apartment complexes in Columbus set a minimum credit score (often somewhere around 600–650) just to qualify to apply, regardless of your income. It's not fair that a number decides this much, but knowing that it does is what lets you actually plan around it instead of getting blindsided by it.

How to Check Yours for Free (The Only Legitimate Way)

annualcreditreport.com is the only site authorized by federal law to give you a free credit report from all three bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Anything else advertising a "free credit score" is usually trying to sign you up for a paid subscription afterward. Beyond your official report, most banking apps (Telhio, Wright-Patt, and Huntington all offer this) show you a free, regularly updated score estimate right inside the app, which is a good way to track your progress month to month without pulling a full report every time.

How to Actually Start Building Credit From Zero

This is the part most people never get walked through: you can't get approved for most regular credit cards with no credit history, and you can't build credit history without a credit card or loan, a real catch-22. Two real products exist specifically to break that loop:

  • A secured credit card, like Telhio's Secured Visa or Wright-Patt's Share Secured Credit Card. You put down a deposit (often as low as $200–$500), which becomes your credit limit, and the card behaves like a completely normal credit card everywhere else. Use it, pay it off in full every month, and it reports to the credit bureaus exactly like an unsecured card would.
  • A credit-builder loan or "first-time user" credit card, like Wright-Patt's First Time User Credit Card, is often designed specifically for people with no credit history at all, sometimes with a lower limit and more guidance built in.

The honest version of the strategy: open one of these, put one small recurring bill on it (a streaming subscription, gas), set up autopay for the full balance every single month, and don't touch it otherwise. That's the whole strategy for the first year.

Myth vs. Fact

Myth: "Checking your own credit score hurts it." Fact: Checking your own score is called a "soft inquiry" and has zero effect. Only a lender checking it to approve you for something (a "hard inquiry") can cause a small, temporary dip.

Myth: "You need to carry a balance and pay interest to build credit." Fact: Paying your full statement balance every month builds credit just as well — usually better — and costs you nothing in interest. Carrying a balance is not required and mostly just costs you money.

Myth: "One missed payment isn't a big deal." Fact: Since payment history is 35% of your score, even one payment reported 30+ days late can cause a real, lasting drop. Setting up autopay for at least the minimum is the single easiest way to make this a non-issue.

Get Started This Week

  • Link: annualcreditreport.com, your one legitimate source for a free credit report from all three bureaus
  • Link: consumerfinance.gov, the federal government's plain-language guide to how credit scores work
  • Telhio Secured Visa (telhio.org) or Wright-Patt's Share Secured Credit Card / First Time User Credit Card (wpcu.coop); real, local starting products built for exactly this situation
  • Your banking app's built-in credit score tracker; check what Telhio, Wright-Patt, or Huntington already show you for free before signing up for anything else
