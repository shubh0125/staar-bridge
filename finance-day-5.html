How to File Your Taxes

Step 1: Gather Your Documents First

Don't start a return with half your documents; you'll get stuck midway and lose momentum. See our companion document, the full Tax Document Checklist, for every possible form you might need. At minimum, for a first-time simple filer, you need:

  • A government-issued photo ID
  • Your Social Security number (card, if you have it handy)
  • Every W-2 you received (one per employer; if you worked two jobs this year, you need both)
  • Your bank account and routing number, if you want your refund by direct deposit (much faster than a mailed check)

Step 2: Choose Where You're Filing

  • IRS Free File (irs.gov/freefile): If you earned under $89,000 in 2025, this gives you free, guided, name-brand tax software directly through the IRS. Best if you're comfortable doing this yourself on a computer.
  • VITA (Volunteer Income Tax Assistance: Free, in-person help from an IRS-certified volunteer who does this with you. Best for your very first time filing, or if anything about your situation feels complicated (multiple jobs, any 1099 income, questions about the foster youth EITC exception covered in Part 2).
  • IRS Direct File: A newer, simpler option to file straight through the IRS with no third-party software at all, if you qualify based on your state and income type.

If you're unsure, start with VITA. Having a real person catch a mistake before you submit is worth more than the extra convenience of doing it alone.

Step 3: Where You Actually Create an Account

This step works completely differently depending on which option you picked in Step 2, and the confusion here is exactly why so many people give up before starting.

If you're using IRS Free File:

  1. Go directly to irs.gov/freefile: type this into your browser yourself. Do not Google "free tax filing" and click the first result – paid companies buy ads that look almost identical to the free option, and landing on their regular commercial site instead of through the IRS gateway means you may not actually get the free version.
  2. On that page, click "Use Free File Guided Tax Software."
  3. Use the "Find Your Trusted Partner" tool; it asks for your age, state (Ohio), and rough income, then shows you which of the IRS's roughly 8 partner companies (names like TaxSlayer, FreeTaxUSA, and OLT) you qualify for. The IRS itself doesn't make the software these companies do, for free, as part of an agreement with the IRS.
  4. Click through to your chosen partner. You'll leave irs.gov and land on that company's own website (This is normal, not a mistake)
  5. On the partner's site, create an account the same way you would for any website: an email address and a password.
  6. Some partners (OLT and FreeTaxUSA, for example) also let you file your Ohio state return for free in the same account, worth checking before you start.

If you're using VITA:

You don't create an account at all. You show up to a VITA site in person (or connect virtually through GetYourRefund.org, a partner service), and a real, IRS-certified volunteer prepares and files the return with you, using their own software on their own computer. This is the simplest option account-wise, because there's nothing for you to set up.

If you're using IRS Direct File:

Availability changes year to year and by state, so check irs.gov directly each filing season to see if it's open where you live before counting on this option.

Step 4: Enter Your Personal Information

Whichever path you used to get here, the software will now ask for your name, date of birth, and Social Security number. Enter these exactly as they appear on your Social Security card; a nickname instead of your legal name, or a single typo in your SSN, is the single most common reason a return gets delayed or rejected. Double-check this screen before moving on.

You'll be asked your filing status. You'll also be asked if anyone can claim you as a dependent. If you're financially independent and nobody (a former foster parent, a relative) is claiming you on their own return, the answer is no.

Step 5: Enter Your W-2, Box by Box

Your W-2 has numbered boxes; the software will ask for them in order. The ones that matter most:

  • Box 1: your total taxable wages for the year (this is your "gross income" for tax purposes)
  • Box 2: how much federal income tax was already withheld from your paychecks
  • Box 17: how much Ohio state income tax was withheld, if applicable

Enter these exactly as printed; don't round, don't estimate. If you worked more than one job, you'll repeat this step for each W-2.

Step 6: Enter Any Other Income

If you did any gig work, freelance work, or received a 1099 form of any kind, this is where you enter it. If you only had W-2 income, you'll skip this step entirely.

Step 7: Answer the Credit and Deduction Questions

The software will ask a series of yes/no questions to check what you qualify for. Answer honestly and don't rush through this section; this is exactly where the standard deduction gets applied automatically, and where the Earned Income Tax Credit questions appear, including whether you're a qualified former foster youth (covered in detail in Part 2 of this series). If a question is confusing, this is a good moment to pause and ask a VITA volunteer rather than guess.

Step 8: Choose Direct Deposit

Enter your bank account and routing number if you want your refund deposited directly; this is typically weeks faster than waiting for a paper check in the mail. Double-check the numbers; a typo here can send your refund to the wrong account entirely.

Step 9: Review Before You Submit

Every filing software gives you a summary screen before final submission. Actually read it — check that your name, SSN, and income numbers match your documents exactly. This is your last chance to catch a mistake before it's filed.

Step 10: File and Save Your Confirmation

Once you submit, you'll get a confirmation (usually an email or a confirmation number on screen) that your return was accepted. Save this. If anything comes up later, this is your proof you filed.

Step 11: Track Your Refund

Use the IRS's own "Where's My Refund?" tool at irs.gov, available about 24 hours after e-filing. You'll need your SSN, filing status, and exact refund amount to check the status. Note: If you're claiming the EITC, federal law requires the IRS to hold those refunds until mid-February regardless of when you file; this isn't a delay specific to you; it's true for everyone claiming that credit.

If Something Feels Off After You File

If you get a letter from the IRS after filing, don't panic and don't ignore it; most notices are routine (confirming identity, requesting a missing document) rather than a sign of a serious problem. Bring any IRS letter to a VITA site or contact Legal Aid of Southeast and Central Ohio (LASCO), many Legal Aid offices have a Low-Income Taxpayer Clinic that helps for free with exactly this kind of situation.

Get Started This Week

  • Link: irs.gov/freefile: start your return here if filing yourself
  • Link: irs.gov/individuals/free-tax-return-preparation: find a VITA site near Columbus for in-person help
  • Link: irs.gov/refunds: track your refund once filed
  • Our companion Tax Document Checklist: gather everything on it before you start
