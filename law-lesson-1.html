Tenant Rights

Have you ever bought a house? If you’re reading this article, I’m assuming not. So, let’s picture a scenario. You found an apartment. The rent is management. The landlord seems nice enough. All that’s left is signing your name…right?

That’s the part where you should take a moment and pause and remind yourself that, once you sign a lease, it's a real legal contract — the same kind of thing as a car loan or a phone contract, just for your home. And in Ohio, the second you sign, you and your landlord both get a set of specific rights enforced by court. Almost all of these rights can be reviewed in one part of state law: Ohio Revised Code Chapter 5321. But, don’t just go ahead and read the whole thing–it’s long. You don’t need that much information–you just need to know the five rules below, because they're the ones that come up the most for someone renting for the first time.

Here's what to check before you sign, what your landlord legally owes you once you move in, and what to do if something goes wrong–including the exact people to contact if it does.

1. Read the lease before you sign it–ALL of it

I know it’s boring, but do it anyway. Before you sign anything, always make sure you know:

  • The exact rent amount and the due date, and what happens if you're even a few days late. Some leases charge a late fee starting day one; others give you a short (or long) grace period. This should be written down or discussed before you sign, not something to figure out after.
  • How long you're locked in for; a one-year lease means you generally can't just leave in month four without owing money. A month-to-month lease is more flexible but can also end faster (see the notice section below).
  • Who pays for what–is water included? Who mows the lawn or shovels snow in winter? Put it in writing, never assume.
  • The security deposit amount and what could get money taken out of it.
  • Rules on guests, pets, and having someone move in with you, if any of that matters to you.

If the landlord says something like "don't worry, I'll fix that before you move in" but it's not in the lease–always get them to text or email it to you. A spoken promise is basically impossible to prove later if there's a disagreement. (Note that there is one exception written into Ohio law: even a landlord who never gives you a written lease at all still has to give you their name and address in writing when you move in. But that's the bare minimum, not the standard. You should always get more in writing when you can.)

2. Your landlord owes you a livable home

Under ORC 5321.04, every Ohio landlord has to do certain things whether or not the lease mentions them. These are things that you shouldn’t have to ask for, as the law already requires them. These are things you shouldn’t have to ask for, the law already requires them:

  • Follow local health and safety codes.
  • Make repairs needed to keep the place actually livable (Classic example: working heater in the winter).
  • Keep shared spaces like hallways and stairwells safe and clean.
  • Keep the plumbing, heat, electrical, and any appliances they gave you (stove, fridge) in working order.
  • Give you at least 24 hours' notice before entering your apartment, except in an emergency like a fire or burst pipe.

This is called the implied warranty of habitability. It's implemented into every Ohio lease automatically. Your landlord can't get you to sign it away, even if the lease has a clause trying to.

If something isn't getting fixed–say, no working heat in December–you have real options under ORC 5321.07, like paying your rent into a court account instead of directly to the landlord (called "rent escrow") until it's fixed, or in some cases ending the lease early. But these steps have a specific order and specific notice requirements, and doing them wrong can backfire. This is exactly the kind of situation to call Legal Aid about before you act, not after—the call is free.

3. Security deposits: the 30-day rule

This is the part people get burned on most, so here's the plain version. Say you paid a $500 security deposit when you moved in.

  • Under ORC 5321.16, your landlord has to give that money back within 30 days after you move out. Not "eventually" — 30 days.
  • If they keep any of it, they have to send you a written, itemized list — basically a receipt — showing exactly what they took the money for.
  • They can only take money for actual damage, not for normal aging. A carpet that's a little worn after a year is normal. A carpet with a burn hole is damage. Regular scuffs, faded paint, and worn spots don't count against you.
  • They generally can't just keep your deposit because you owe back rent. Unpaid rent has its own separate legal process — the deposit isn't a shortcut around it.
  • If you paid more than $50 (or one month's rent, whichever is bigger) and stayed six months or longer, that deposit is supposed to earn a small amount of interest.

The one thing you have to do: give your landlord a forwarding address in writing when you move out — a text or email works, just keep proof you sent it. Skip this and you weaken your own case if the deposit doesn't come back on time.

If your landlord blows past the 30 days or keeps money for reasons that don't add up, you can take them to court for it — and if you gave that written forwarding address, you can potentially get back double what they wrongfully kept, plus your attorney's fees if you have one. And here's the part people don't realize: you don't need a lawyer to do this. Deposit disputes under $3,000 can be filed in small claims court by you, on your own, for a small filing fee.

Quick timeline:

  1. You move out → give your landlord a forwarding address in writing.
  2. Their 30-day clock starts.
  3. Either they return your deposit (full amount, or an itemized list of what they kept) — or they miss the deadline / don't give a valid reason.
  4. If it's the second one, you can file in small claims court for up to double what they owe you.

4. You can't just leave whenever, and neither can they

If you're month-to-month, either side can end things, but Ohio law (ORC 5321.17) requires at least 30 days' written notice first — from you or from them. If you're on a one-year lease, you're generally locked in for the full year unless the lease allows an early out or your landlord seriously fails to hold up their end.

If your landlord wants you out over something you did that affects health or safety, they usually have to give you a 30-day notice to fix it or move out (ORC 5321.11) first. They can't just show up one day and change the locks — that's illegal, no matter what the lease says.

5. You can't legally be punished for speaking up

A lot of first-time renters stay quiet about a broken heater or a mold problem because they're scared the landlord will retaliate — raise the rent, refuse to renew, or try to evict them out of spite. Ohio law has your back here: ORC 5321.02 makes it illegal for a landlord to punish you for exercising your rights, like asking for a repair or reporting a code violation. If they try it, that retaliation is itself something you can raise in court as a defense.

Before you sign, do these three things

  1. Get every promise in writing — verbal assurances don't hold up.
  2. Walk the unit and document its condition with photos or video before you move in, so you have proof for when you move out.
  3. Save your landlord's name, address, and contact info somewhere you won't lose it — you'll need it if a dispute ever comes up.

You don't have to figure this out alone, and you don't have to pay for a lawyer to get real answers:

  • Ohio Legal Help — a free, plain-language guide to tenant rights across the state.
  • Legal Aid of Southeast and Central Ohio (LASCO), formerly Legal Aid Society of Columbus — free civil legal help for renters in Franklin County and beyond. Apply online at lasco.org or call their intake line at (888) 246-4420.

A lease is one of the first adult contracts most people sign — and it's completely reasonable to want backup before you do. Knowing these five rules won't make you a lawyer, but it will make you a much harder person to take advantage of.
