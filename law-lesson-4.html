What Actually Makes a Contract Binding

A gym membership. A phone plan. A streaming subscription. An apartment lease you already covered in article one. All of these are contracts — and the second you sign (or, in some cases, click "I agree"), you've entered a legal agreement a court will enforce, whether you read it or not.

Here's the good news: you don't need a law degree to protect yourself. You need to know what makes a contract binding, when you actually have a legal right to cancel, and where the fine-print traps usually hide.

1. What actually makes a contract binding

A contract doesn't need to be a scary legal document to count. In plain terms, you have a binding contract when three things are true:

  • An offer was made — "sign up for 12 months of service at $40/month."
  • You accepted it — you signed, clicked agree, or otherwise said yes.
  • Something of value changed hands on both sides — you pay money, they give you a service or product.

That's it. A text message agreeing to terms can count. A checkbox you clicked without reading can count. The contract doesn't have to be fair to be binding — it just has to meet those three things. That's exactly why reading before you sign matters so much more than it seems like it should.

2. The cancellation clause is the first thing to find

Before you look at price, features, or anything else, find the answer to one question: how do I get out of this if I need to? Specifically:

  • How much notice do you have to give to cancel?
  • Is there a cancellation fee, and how much?
  • Does cancelling require a phone call, a letter, or can you do it online?
  • Is there a minimum commitment period you can't get out of at all?

If you can't find the cancellation terms, or they're vague, that itself is a red flag.

3. Auto-renewals: don't assume there's a safety net

You might have heard that companies now have to make it "as easy to cancel as it was to sign up." That was a real federal rule the FTC tried to put in place — but a federal appeals court struck it down in 2025, and as of now, it's not in effect. That means for most Ohio consumers, there's currently no broad law guaranteeing an easy one-click cancellation for subscriptions.

What this means practically: the contract's own auto-renewal clause is what actually governs you. Before you sign anything that renews automatically — a gym membership, a streaming bundle, a phone contract — find out exactly how far in advance you have to cancel to stop the renewal, and mark that date somewhere you'll actually see it.

4. When you do have an automatic legal right to cancel

There are two specific situations where Ohio and federal law step in and give you a real cancellation right, whether the contract mentions it or not:

The FTC's "cooling-off rule" gives you 3 business days to cancel a sale of $25 or more if it happened somewhere other than the seller's normal place of business — your home, your dorm, a hotel conference room, a pop-up table. It does not apply to purchases made entirely online, by mail, or by phone.

Ohio's Prepaid Entertainment Contracts Act covers gym memberships, dance studio lessons, martial arts training, and dating services specifically. If you sign one of these in Ohio, you get 3 business days to cancel for a full refund (the business can only keep up to $10 as an expense fee). A few more real protections built into this law:

  • The contract can't run longer than 3 years.
  • If you move 25 miles or more away from the gym (or it relocates 25+ miles away from you), you're entitled to a refund for the time you have left.
  • Cancellation has to be accepted in writing — by mail or in person.

Outside of these two specific situations, most everyday contracts don't come with an automatic legal escape hatch — which is exactly why finding the contract's own cancellation clause matters so much.

Quick check: did you sign at your home/workplace for $25+, or is it an Ohio gym, dance, martial arts, or dating contract? If yes, you have a 3-business-day cancellation right by law. If not, no automatic right applies — the contract's own clause is your only protection.

5. Real fine-print traps to watch for

  • Automatic renewal into a new full term — some gym and service contracts renew you into another full year instead of month-to-month if you miss the cancellation window.
  • "Cancel by phone only" clauses, when you signed up online in two clicks — a classic way companies make leaving harder than joining.
  • Vague "verbal promises" — if a salesperson tells you something isn't in the contract but promises it anyway, it doesn't count unless it's written down.
  • Hidden fees stacked on top of the advertised price — activation fees, maintenance fees, early termination fees.

Before you sign, do this

  1. Find the cancellation clause first — before price, before features.
  2. Get every verbal promise in writing — a text or email from the seller works.
  3. Check for an auto-renewal clause, and if there is one, mark your cancel-by date somewhere you'll actually see it.
  4. Keep a copy of everything you sign, including the date.

Where to get real help

  • consumer.ftc.gov — the FTC's plain-language guide to contracts, cancellation rights, and the cooling-off rule.
  • ohioattorneygeneral.gov/consumer — Ohio-specific consumer contract protections, plus a real complaint portal.
  • OhioProtects.org, or call 800-282-0515 — the Ohio Attorney General's consumer complaint line.

Reading a contract before you sign it isn't difficult or paranoid. It’s a good habit–it's the one habit that decides whether you're in control of an agreement or stuck inside one.

This article is for general educational purposes and isn't a substitute for legal advice. Laws and consumer protections can change — always verify current details with the FTC or the Ohio Attorney General's office before relying on them.
