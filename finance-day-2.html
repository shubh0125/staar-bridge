Budgeting and Building Your Safety Net

As of January 2026, Ohio's minimum wage is $11.00 an hour. Work 35 hours a week at that rate, and you're bringing home roughly $1,540 a month before taxes, closer to $1,340–$1,400 after taxes, depending on your withholding. That number isn't small, but it also isn't a lot once rent, food, a phone bill, and transportation are all pulling from the same pool. The difference between that paycheck covering you comfortably and that paycheck running out by the 20th of the month almost always comes down to one skill: budgeting. And the difference between surviving a flat tire and having it turn into a real crisis comes down to a second, closely related skill: having something saved before you need it.

The 50/30/20 Framework

This is the starting framework we recommend, because it's simple enough to actually stick to and flexible enough to adjust once real bills come in:

  • 50% Needs: Rent, utilities, groceries, transportation, minimum debt payments. The non-negotiables.
  • 30% Wants: Eating out, streaming subscriptions, hobbies, clothes beyond the basics.
  • 20%: Savings & extra debt payoff. Emergency fund, future goals, paying down anything beyond the minimum.

On that $1,340/month take-home example: that's roughly $670 for needs, $400 for wants, and $270 for savings. In a city like Columbus, $670 for needs is tight if you're renting alone—which is why most people this age split rent with a roommate, or why the needs percentage temporarily runs higher than 50% until income grows. The framework is a target to work toward, not a rule that breaks if reality doesn't match it exactly.

What This Actually Looks Like on Paper

Say you're working 35 hours a week at $11/hour in Columbus, splitting a two-bedroom apartment with a roommate:

NeedsRent (half of $1,100)$550
NeedsUtilities (split)$60
NeedsGroceries$250
NeedsPhone plan$45

WantsEating out / going out$150

That adds up to roughly $1,330 of a $1,340 take-home check. The difference between this budget and just "spending until the money's gone" is that every dollar already has a job before the month even starts.

When Your Income Isn't the Same Every Month

A lot of budgeting advice quietly assumes you get the same paycheck every two weeks. If you're hourly, especially in retail or food service, that's often not true; one month you get 35 hours a week, the next you get 22 because the schedule changed. Here's the fix: budget off your lowest realistic month, not your average or your best one. Look at your last 2–3 months of pay stubs, find the smallest one, and build your needs and wants categories around that number. Any month you make more than that becomes extra money that goes straight to savings or a short-term goal, not new spending. This one shift is what keeps a good month from quietly funding the mistakes of a bad one.

Sinking Funds: Saving for Costs That Aren't Monthly, But Are Coming

A sinking fund is money you save a little at a time, on purpose, for an expense you know is coming but doesn't show up every month — car registration, a holiday gift, a broken phone screen, winter tires. The trick is to work backward: if car registration costs $80 a year, set aside about $7 a month so the bill doesn't blindside you later. A few sinking funds worth starting early: a car repair fund, a "something broke" fund for whatever you rent or own, and a holiday/gift fund if that's part of your life. None of these need to be $10–$20 a month per category is a completely reasonable start.

Your Emergency Fund: The One That's Different From the Rest

A sinking fund is for an expense you can predict. An emergency fund is for the one you can't predict- for example, a sudden car repair, an unexpected medical bill, a week without hours at work. This is the single most protective thing you can build financially, because without it, an emergency doesn't just cost money; it can turn into a predatory loan, a missed rent payment, or debt that takes years to climb out of.

A realistic starting goal is $500–$1,000, not the "3–6 months of expenses" number often quoted for people much further along financially. That number is a long-term goal, not a starting line, and treating it as the minimum before you start saving anything is exactly why people never start. Even $150 a month, like in the sample budget above, gets you to $1,000 in well under a year.

Where to actually keep it: a separate savings account from your everyday checking – not because it needs to be complicated, but because money you don't see every day is money you're less tempted to spend on something that isn't actually an emergency. Telhio Credit Union and Wright-Patt Credit Union both offer basic savings accounts with no meaningful barrier to opening one alongside a checking account, and Huntington's Premier Savings account requires just a $50 minimum deposit if you'd rather keep everything at one bank.

Tools to Actually Build This, Not Just Read About It

You don't need an app subscription to budget well. A few free options:

  • A free Google Sheets budget template: search "budget template" in the Google Sheets template gallery; several free, pre-built options let you plug in your own numbers immediately
  • EveryDollar (everydollar.com): a free, beginner-friendly budgeting app with a straightforward zero-based budgeting approach
  • Your bank or credit union's own app: Telhio, Wright-Patt, and Huntington all offer built-in spending trackers that categorize your transactions automatically, which is often the easiest starting point since it's already tracking money you're spending anyway

When the Budget Doesn't Work the First Month

It usually doesn't, and that's not failure; it's information. If you run out of money in the "wants" category by the 20th, that's not a sign to give up; it's a sign that category needs to shrink and "needs" needs a closer look next month.

The One Rule Worth Remembering

Budget for savings the same way you budget for rent: as a fixed cost, not as "whatever's left over." If savings only happens with leftover money, it rarely happens, because there's rarely anything left over by accident. Even $20-$50 a month, moved automatically into a separate savings account the day you get paid, builds real momentum faster than people expect.

Get Started This Week

  • Link: consumerfinance.gov/your-money-your-goal: the federal government's own free budgeting toolkit, no account required
  • A free budgeting spreadsheet template from the Google Sheets template gallery; copy it, plug in your real numbers, done in 15 minutes
  • Your own last 2-3 pay stubs; the real starting point for any budget is your real income, not a guess or an average
  • A separate savings account (Telhio, Wright-Patt, or Huntington all work) opened specifically for your emergency fund, out of sight, out of impulse-spending reach
